Prefer to read?

Why test a message before it goes public? Because the meaning a team intends is not always the meaning a reader takes away.

Show the actual draft to people it is meant to reach. Before explaining it, ask them to describe what it means in their own words.

Then ask what they would do next. A message can sound appealing while leaving the date, location or required action unclear.

Imagine a notice that says, expanded access begins next month. Does that mean longer hours, a new location, or different services? This is an illustrative example, not a client finding.

Separate confusion from disagreement. Revise missing information, and test again when the decision warrants it. Qualitative feedback explains interpretations; it is not a population-wide vote.

Direct answer

What is message research?

Message research puts draft language and the issue behind it in front of intended audiences, asks what they think it means and uses their reasoning to guide revisions.

The first question is not simply, “Do you like it?” A participant may like a phrase but understand it differently from the team that wrote it. Message testing research should explore meaning, credibility, confusion and what the wording leaves out.

Qualitative findings explain how and why people respond. They do not by themselves estimate how many people across a population will agree, vote or change behavior. For a broader method discussion, see PRC's qualitative research studies guide.

Interpretation signals

Listen for more than approval.

These signals can help a team decide whether a draft needs explanation, reframing or another round of testing.

Meaning

What do people think it says?

Ask participants to explain the wording in their own language before offering a preferred interpretation.

Credibility

What feels believable?

Explore the assumptions and lived context behind trust or skepticism, not just a yes-or-no reaction.

Missing context

What question remains?

Look for facts, definitions or boundaries that a public explanation may need to supply.

For public agencies

How can an agency test a public-service message?

Put the real draft in front of people it is meant to reach, ask them to explain it in their own words and identify what they would do next. Compare those interpretations with what the agency intended before revising the notice.

01 / Meaning

What is changing?

Ask which service, place, date or action people believe the wording describes. A familiar term inside an agency may be unclear outside it.

02 / Action

What would someone do next?

Test whether the draft makes the next step understandable, including where to find official details or ask a question. Do not let a research discussion substitute for official eligibility or policy guidance.

03 / Revision

What needs clarification?

Record confusion and differing interpretations, revise the draft and consider another test when the consequences of misunderstanding are high.

For example, a city changing service hours could test whether residents understand which office is affected, when the change begins and where to confirm current hours. This is an illustrative research scenario, not a PRC project claim. The CDC's message-testing guidance provides independent context for getting audience feedback on clarity and usefulness.

Before you commission

Bring the decision, audience and draft.

You do not need a perfect research plan to start. A useful inquiry explains the choice your team faces and the language you are considering.

What the brief should make clear

Which issue or message is under review? Who is meant to understand it? What materials exist? When must the team decide? What will it do with findings? Who approves the final language? Avoid sending confidential participant data or unreleased material in an initial form inquiry.

Practical questions

Before the message goes public.

Planning questions before you test a message.

Is message testing the same as polling?

No. Qualitative message research explores interpretation and reasons through conversation. A population estimate or percentage requires an appropriate quantitative design, which is not claimed as a PRC service here.

When should a team test language?

Testing is most useful while the team can still revise its draft and before a consequential rollout. The exact timing depends on the decision, audience, review process and materials available.

Who should participate?

The intended audience and people affected by the issue should guide selection. The study brief should identify important differences and what a small qualitative sample cannot represent.

What should the team receive?

Agree the outputs in scope. Our work can include findings, interpretation and recommendations. A useful report should distinguish what participants expressed from the team's conclusions and explain limitations.

Can this connect to community listening?

Yes, when the project calls for both understanding an issue and hearing from affected people. Community engagement research focuses on participation and how input informs a public decision.

Practical decision aid

Test the next step, not just whether people like it.

A draft may sound clear to its authors while leaving the reader unsure what to do. Use a small interpretation exercise while the wording can still change. The exercise below is illustrative, not a report of PRC client findings.

Show the actual draft

Present the language in the format people would encounter. Avoid explaining the intended meaning first; otherwise the response may reflect your explanation rather than the draft.

Ask for the next action

Ask the reader to explain the meaning and describe what they would do next. Note where they need a date, definition, exception or source of official information.

Choose the revision

Separate misunderstanding from disagreement. Clarify missing information without treating a favorable reaction as proof that the message will change behavior.

What to clarify before commissioning

Example: an office notice says “Expanded access begins next month.” A reader may need to know which office, which services, the start date and whether an appointment is still required. Record those information gaps, revise the notice and retest when the risk warrants it. This example demonstrates a research task; it does not establish any actual service change.
