What can an in-depth interview reveal that a group may miss? It creates space to follow one person’s experience, reasoning and context.

Begin with a specific example. Ask what happened, what the person understood, and what they did next. Use open questions instead of suggesting the answer.

A discussion guide keeps the research focused, while follow-up questions explore details the team may not have anticipated. It is a guide, not a script for the participant.

Interviews can suit complex, specialized or personal topics. They still need clear consent and data-handling arrangements, and their findings are not a population percentage.

If a larger study already exists, specify whether the work covers interviewing, analysis, or the whole study. Clear responsibilities help the research team use the contribution correctly.

Direct answer

What are in-depth interviews?

In-depth interviews are individual, guided conversations designed to explore how a person understands an experience, service or decision. The moderator asks open questions and follows the participant's reasoning.

An interview is especially useful when a topic is complex, specialized or too personal for a group setting. It can reveal the sequence behind a decision and language a person uses to explain it. Findings are qualitative and should not be presented as a representative survey result.

Scope a project

What to include in an interview brief.

Describe the decision, target audience, what you need to understand, any study materials already developed, expected timing and how findings will be used. Say whether you need study design, interviews, analysis or a defined combination.

Participant information needs care.

Discuss consent, recording, data handling and sensitive subject matter during planning. Do not submit participant names or confidential records in the initial form.

Buyer questions

Interview questions, answered.

When are interviews better than focus groups?

When individual circumstances, specialized knowledge or privacy matter more than observing group interaction. A combined study may use both for different questions.

How many interviews are enough?

No universal count fits every decision. The plan should reflect audience differences, depth, resources and what conclusions the study can responsibly support.

Can the interviewer follow unexpected answers?

Yes. A semi-structured guide keeps the inquiry aligned while allowing relevant follow-up. Leading the participant toward a preferred answer would weaken the work.

Does PRC only conduct full studies?

No. PRC's documented national prescriber work was interview support within another firm's larger study. Discuss the exact division of work for your project.

Practical decision aid

Move from a general opinion to a specific experience.

A useful interview can follow what happened, what a person understood at the time and what shaped the next step. Open prompts invite detail without telling the participant which answer the researcher prefers.

Start with an event

Invite a recent relevant example rather than a general account of what normally happens. Ask where the experience began and what the person was trying to accomplish.

Follow the sequence

Ask what happened next, what information was available and which options were considered. Clarify an unfamiliar term instead of assuming the interviewer and participant mean the same thing.

Compare responsibly

Use a consistent topic guide while allowing relevant follow-up. Compare accounts and meaningful differences without turning the number of mentions into a representative percentage.

What to clarify before commissioning

Agree responsibilities before scheduling. A provider moderating an existing study may not own recruitment, the guide, analysis or the final report. Confirm permitted recording, access to files, retention and any confidentiality limits through the study’s actual arrangements. These are scoping questions, not a promise that one handling plan suits every project.

Planning guide

What to include in an in-depth interview brief

Use in-depth interviews to understand how individuals experience a service, make a choice or work through a sensitive issue. A one-to-one format gives the interviewer time to follow the participant’s own account and ask about specific events.

Recruit for experiences the study needs to compare

Identify the experiences that matter. For example, if a program wants to learn why people stop an application, hear from people who stopped at different steps as well as people who completed it. Avoid screening in only the people easiest for staff to reach.

Write questions that invite a real example

Begin with a recent experience. Ask what happened next, what the person expected, who they asked for help and where the process changed. Leave room for follow-up questions. GOV.UK’s guidance on in-depth interviews explains how a discussion guide and open questions can support this approach.

Agree on how the team will compare interviews

Ask for a summary organized around the research question, recurring themes, important exceptions and the experiences of people the study could not reach. Decide before the first interview who can access recordings or quotes and what details will be removed from shared outputs.
