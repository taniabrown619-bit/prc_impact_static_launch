Prefer to read?

When is a focus group the right research choice? When hearing people respond to one another can help explain a shared experience or idea.

A moderator asks open questions and explores the language, differences and assumptions that emerge. The interaction is part of the evidence.

A focus group does not tell you what percentage of an entire population agrees. And a private or sensitive experience may be better discussed one to one.

Before commissioning, clarify who will recruit, moderate, analyze and report. Agree the audience, research question, consent arrangements and what the findings need to inform.

Think of the group as a conversation lens, not a vote. Choose it because the discussion can illuminate the decision, not because a fixed number of sessions sounds convenient.

Direct answer

What is focus group research?

A focus group is a moderated discussion with selected participants about a defined topic. The researcher asks open questions, observes how perspectives interact and interprets recurring ideas and differences.

Group discussion can reveal shared language, social expectations and points of disagreement. It cannot, by itself, tell you what percentage of a whole population holds a view. If someone needs space to discuss a private or sensitive experience, an individual interview may fit better.

PRC offers focus groups within broader qualitative research studies as well as focused research support. If you are comparing a focus group research company, ask who will design the guide, recruit participants, moderate the discussion, analyze differences and explain the limits of the findings. This page is for organizations commissioning research, not people seeking paid focus group participation.

Design a focus group study that answers a real decision

A focus group study uses moderated group interaction to explore how people understand an issue, compare experiences or react to materials. Define what the interaction can reveal before deciding how to recruit or how many sessions to run.

01Focus group research design

When is group discussion worth using?

Choose a group when participants’ responses to one another can reveal shared language, exceptions or reasons for disagreement. For example, when a service team needs to understand why people read the same instructions differently, a group lets participants compare how they reached their interpretations. Ask each person to give an initial reading before the group discusses it.

If the decision depends on confidential individual experiences, start with in-depth interviews. If you need population estimates, plan a separate quantitative step. CDC guidance on interviews and focus groups.

02Focus group recruitment and facilitation

How do you design groups that make differences easier to hear?

Define criteria around the research question. To study a service handoff, you might separate participants who used it for the first time from those familiar with the process. That creates space for newer users to describe what confused them. Explain each group’s purpose in the recruitment plan and record which perspectives the screener missed.

Plan what the moderator will do when a few voices dominate. Options include writing down initial responses or hearing from participants in turn before open discussion. If the team already owns recruitment and analysis, consider moderator support.

03Focus group analysis and reporting

What should a focus group report include?

Ask for a focus group findings report organized by research question, with evidence from more than one session where available and a record of competing interpretations. A reader should be able to distinguish a theme heard across several groups from a point raised by one person. Both may matter, but they call for different follow-up.

For a service redesign, map each finding to the step it affects, the change worth considering and the evidence still needed. Agree before the sessions on how to handle excerpts and recordings. GOV.UK guidance on qualitative focus group studies.

More questions before you start

What should we decide before choosing online or in-person groups?

Check participants’ access, the sensitivity of the discussion and whether participants need to handle or see materials. Identify who will help with technology or accessibility. Choose the format that supports the discussion you need.

Should we keep adding groups until everyone agrees?

Use follow-up groups to investigate meaningful differences or reach people you missed. A result can be useful when people disagree, as long as the team can explain where the disagreement appears and what it means for the decision.

Pause and reconsider

Private disclosure

Do not ask a group setting to carry personal topics that participants may be unwilling or unable to discuss safely. Consider interviews instead.

What a buyer should ask

Who will define the research question, recruit participants, moderate, analyze and prepare the report? A session count alone does not answer those questions.

If your team already owns a study, discuss whether it needs full research design or only moderation and analysis support.

Commissioning brief

What belongs in the first conversation?

Name the decision, the people affected, what is already known, what is still uncertain and the date by which findings must be useful. If a draft message or service concept exists, explain what can still change. When personal context matters more than group interaction, compare in-depth interview research before choosing a format.

Plan participant care.

Discuss accessibility, language, confidentiality and recording or consent arrangements during scope design. Do not send participant lists or sensitive records through an initial inquiry form.

Buyer questions

Focus group questions, answered.

How many focus groups do we need?

There is no universal number. The choice depends on distinct audiences, the decision, depth needed and resources. Define what each group adds rather than starting with a fixed count.

Can a focus group represent everyone?

No. It can reveal how selected participants explain and discuss an issue; it is not a probability survey or a population percentage.

Can groups be virtual or in person?

PRC's verified work includes virtual and in-person moderation. Choose a format after considering participants, access, discussion needs and logistics.

What if our topic is sensitive?

Consider whether an individual interview would give participants more privacy. The study can combine methods when each has a clear purpose.

Practical decision aid

Choose the group for the interaction it can reveal.

Start with the evidence you need, then decide whether people discussing the topic together would add value. A focus group is not automatically the best choice merely because several people are available at once.

Choose a group

Use the interaction when shared language, competing interpretations or discussion of a common experience matters. Ask how participants build on and challenge one another’s accounts.

Choose an interview

Consider individual interviews when a sensitive experience, specialized workflow or power imbalance could make a group discussion less candid. Confidentiality cannot be guaranteed by other participants.

Choose a different design

If the decision needs a defensible estimate of how common a view is, a group discussion alone cannot provide it. Define a suitable measurement design separately.

What to clarify before commissioning

For comparable proposals, ask each research provider to distinguish recruitment, screening, moderation, consent and recording, analysis, reporting and project management. Clarify who supplies facilities or a virtual platform, handles participant arrangements and approves the discussion guide. A low session price is not a complete study scope. No standard project price or fixed group count is promised here.
