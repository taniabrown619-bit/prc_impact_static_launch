Thematic Analysis and Codebook Design

Thematic analysis identifies patterns across interviews or other qualitative data. Use a codebook to label what people describe, compare examples and exceptions across the material, and connect each theme to evidence the decision team can check.

What do you do with a stack of interview transcripts? Think of a codebook as a map legend. It helps you mark what you see the same way as you move through the material.

Imagine a rider who says a transit alert arrived on time, but they couldn’t tell where to transfer. Mark that passage “next step is unclear.” The codebook should say when to use that label and when to leave it out. An alert that came too late needs its own label.

Then compare interviews. If several riders had time to act but didn’t know where to go, the theme might be that the notice gives them time without giving them enough direction. Check the exceptions. One rider might have found a posted map. That gives the team a concrete next step, like testing a notice that names the stop and includes a map.

01 / Define the question

Start with what the decision team needs to learn

In thematic analysis for qualitative research, you read across interviews or other text to identify patterns that answer a research question. A code labels an observation in an excerpt. A theme explains a pattern across related observations and shows why it matters to the decision.

For example, a transit team asking why riders miss appointments may start with codes for alert timing, transfer instructions and rider workarounds. A theme can explain how those observations fit together. Read the CDC Field Epidemiology Manual’s qualitative data chapter for a practical overview of coding and interpretation.

02 / Build the codebook

A qualitative research codebook example

Give each code a name, a definition, a rule for when to use it, a boundary and a sample excerpt. This illustrative codebook uses a question about how riders respond to transit changes. All excerpts on this page are fictional.

Notice arrives too late

Use for
The rider learned of a change too late to adjust.
Leave out
The rider saw the notice in time but could not understand the next step.
Example excerpt
“The alert showed up after I had already left.”

Next step is unclear

Use for
The rider knew there was a change but could not tell where to go or what to do.
Leave out
The route was clear and the rider could not reach it because of another barrier.
Example excerpt
“It said to transfer downtown, but I didn’t know which stop.”

Independent workaround

Use for
The rider describes how they found another way to complete the trip.
Leave out
The rider imagines a possible alternative they have never used.
Example excerpt
“I used the route map at the stop and caught another bus.”

Codebook template. Copy this line for each code and replace the blanks.

Code name ___ · Definition ___ · Use for ___ · Leave out ___ · Example excerpt ID ___ · Last revised ___

If multiple people are coding, ask them to try the first version on the same few excerpts and talk through the differences. Update the definition and revisit earlier excerpts affected by the change.

03 / Code and interpret

From a coded transcript to a useful theme

When coding interview transcripts, choose a passage long enough to preserve its meaning. In this example, “I saw the alert before I left. It said to transfer downtown, but I didn’t know which stop” gets next step is unclear. It does not get notice arrives too late because the rider saw the alert before leaving. A passage can receive two codes if it provides evidence for both.

Compare patterns across interviews. Suppose several riders saw an alert in time but could not tell where to go. The theme could be notices give riders time to act, but omit the detail needed to act. Test that interpretation against a rider who used a posted map to complete the trip. The team could then test an alert that includes a named stop, a map link and a text alternative.

Make the finding traceable. Keep a short memo with the theme, supporting excerpt IDs, an exception, and the decision it can inform. Record whose experiences the study missed. Use counts to help navigate your material and be explicit about whether they count people or excerpts.

Common questions

Do I need to finish the codebook before starting analysis?

Begin with definitions for the concepts you expect to encounter. Use an initial batch of transcripts to add concepts, refine definitions and check whether another analyst interprets the boundaries the same way. Keep a dated record of changes.

Is the most frequently used code the most important finding?

A frequent code shows what appeared often in this material. Consider who raised it, under what conditions and how it bears on the decision. An infrequent accessibility barrier can still prevent someone from using the service.

What can I share without exposing a participant?

Check consent and remove identifying details before sharing quotations. If a detail or combination of details could still identify someone, summarize the finding without the quotation. See in-depth interviews for planning and qualitative research studies for reporting deliverables.
