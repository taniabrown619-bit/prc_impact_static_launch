Prefer to read?

If you want to know why people miss library appointments, who would you interview? It’s tempting to start with the people who show up. But that’s like asking only passengers who caught the bus why others missed it.

Start with the decision you need to make. A library team reviewing appointment reminders might talk to people who booked and attended, people who booked and missed, and people who tried to book and gave up. Those groups can reveal different problems.

Use a short, neutral screener. Ask what happened the last time they tried to book. Then track who was invited and who actually spoke with you. If every interview came from the library’s email list, ask which experiences that list missed. A trusted community partner may be able to share a voluntary invitation.

01 / Plan the sample

How to recruit participants for a research study

Start with the decision the study needs to inform. Write down which experiences could change that decision, how you will identify people with those experiences and which outreach channels could reach them. For a qualitative study, the goal is to hear the range of experiences that matter to the decision.

For example, if a public library wants to change its appointment reminders, interviewing only people who attended will miss the reasons people never showed up. Use the following illustrative recruitment matrix to track who you have heard from and where to recruit next.

Booked and attended
Learn what made the visit possible.
Reach through an opt-in invitation after a visit.
Booked and missed
Learn what got in the way between booking and arrival.
Reach through a permitted, one-time invitation from the booking team.
Tried and gave up
Learn what stopped the booking.
Reach through a community partner or a notice at the booking desk.

Make a simple recruitment brief that records the decision, eligible experiences, target mix, outreach channels, session length, available formats, participation support, incentive if offered and who handles scheduling. Use at least one route beyond your existing email list when that list excludes people you need to hear from. See Digital.gov’s guide to recruiting participants and the UK Government Service Manual’s recruitment brief.

02 / Confirm eligibility

A short participant screening questionnaire

A screener checks whether someone has the experience the study needs. Ask about concrete behavior before opinions. Keep the eligibility rule in your planning notes and use neutral options in the invitation and screener.

  1. Experience. “In the past three months, did you try to book an appointment with the library?” Offer yes, no and unsure.
  2. Path. “What happened the last time?” Offer booked and attended, booked and missed, tried without booking, and something else. Follow up neutrally when someone is unsure.
  3. Participation. “If invited, what times or formats could work for you?” Ask separately whether an accommodation would help. Store scheduling and access information separately from research notes.

Invitation example. “We’re talking with people about what happens when they try to make a library appointment. The conversation will take about 30 minutes. Taking part is optional and will not affect the services you receive.” Add who is conducting the study and how contact details will be used.

03 / Check for gaps

Keep a recruitment tracker

Track how many people were invited, screened, scheduled and interviewed in each experience group. Record the channel as well. If everyone comes from one partner or location, look for a second way to reach the groups you are missing. Keep a short list of unresolved gaps for the findings report.

For sensitive topics, settle consent, permissions and privacy before outreach. Collect the minimum information needed and share the approved privacy information with participants. Use individual interviews when people need privacy to speak freely, or the focus group research guide if interaction among participants is central to the design.

Common questions

How many people should I recruit for qualitative research?

Plan enough interviews or groups to hear the experience categories you identified. Review coverage after the first sessions. If a priority group is absent or an important explanation remains unclear, direct the next round there and report any remaining gaps.

What can I do when the people I need are hard to reach?

Ask a trusted partner to share an opt-in invitation, vary session times, and offer another language or format where practical. Record who was still missing so readers can see whose experiences the study represents.

What should go in a recruiting brief for an outside research partner?

Share your research decision, the required mix of experiences, approved contact channels, scheduling constraints and privacy expectations. The qualitative research studies guide explains how the brief fits into the wider study.
