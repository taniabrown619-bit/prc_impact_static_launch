Qualitative Research for Nonprofits and Coalitions

Qualitative research helps nonprofits and coalitions understand who needs a program, where access breaks down and what to change. Use a needs assessment before designing a response and program evaluation after services are underway.

How do nonprofits and coalitions ask better questions before changing a program? Start with the decision. A needs assessment asks who needs support and where access breaks down. A program evaluation asks how an existing service is working and what the team should test next.

Think of a referral as a relay. A partner may think the handoff is complete when it sends the contact details. The person waiting for a call may see it differently. Ask participants, staff, and partner organizations about the same steps, from referral to first contact and follow-up.

Imagine a coalition whose food support referrals keep stalling. Interview people who reached intake and people who stopped. Check when callbacks actually happened. If missed callbacks emerge as a barrier, test scheduled callbacks with one partner and see what changes.

Use the worksheet to name the decision, pick the evidence, and assign someone to review it. If your organization needs help planning interviews or a qualitative study, talk with us at PRC Impact. Find the guide and start a project at prcimpact.com.

Scope a qualitative nonprofit needs assessment

Start by deciding what you need to assess. A community needs assessment asks what people need, who is missing from current services and which barriers matter. An organizational assessment asks what the nonprofit itself needs, such as staff or systems. If the decision is about who the program reaches, combine existing community data with accounts from participants, former participants and people who never enrolled.

If attendance has fallen, ask people to describe the last time they tried to participate. Their answers can distinguish a scheduling problem from transportation, eligibility instructions or a concern about how they will be treated. Each points to a different response.

Plan nonprofit stakeholder engagement and coalition interviews

Ask each group about the same critical steps, such as referral, first contact and follow-up. Hold separate interviews when one partner funds another, a staff member controls access, or participants may be hesitant to speak openly.

Build an account of where perspectives agree and differ. A partner may consider a referral complete when it sends contact details, while a participant may experience it as unfinished until someone answers. Use that mismatch to define a more useful measure.

Use qualitative evidence in nonprofit program evaluation

Ask whether the program is reaching people as intended, what explains barriers and what should change next. Combine interviews with records your team already has, such as referrals, first appointments and follow-up. Interview people whose experiences differ, including people who did not complete the program.

Keep the explanation and the count separate. Interviews can show how a referral breaks down, and attendance records can show how often an observed step occurs in the records. An evaluation that needs to show whether a program caused an improvement needs a different design.

Agree on a change the coalition can test

Report the pattern, the evidence behind it and where it may differ. Ask which partner can change the process and what support they need. Publish a participant-facing summary when appropriate, keeping confidential details out of it.

For each proposed change, identify the earliest sign it helped and what could go wrong. A new follow-up call might improve referral completion while creating a language access bottleneck if the team does not plan for it.

CDC evaluation questions and design

Planning tool

Nonprofit program evaluation template

  1. Decision and owner

    Complete this sentence: By [date], [owner] will decide whether to [change].

    Example: The program director will decide whether to pilot scheduled callbacks.
  2. Evidence to collect

    Write down a program question, one measure from existing records and whose account you still need.

    Example: How many referrals reached first contact? Interview people whose referrals stopped and the staff who process them.
  3. Review and next step

    Write how you will compare evidence, what you cannot infer and when you will report back.

    Example: Compare first contact before and during the pilot, then ask participants what the pilot still missed.
Worked example

Imagine a coalition where people are referred for food support but rarely finish intake. Interview people who completed it and people who stopped, along with referring staff. If missing callbacks emerge, test a scheduled callback with one partner before changing the entire network.

Questions to consider

Should we interview funders too?

Include a funder when its requirements constrain what partners can change. Ask about those constraints separately so program participants can describe their experience without feeling evaluated.

What if the partner organizations disagree about the problem?

Show each version alongside its evidence and identify a step both can verify. A small workflow test can surface which explanation fits without forcing consensus in the report.

Can a qualitative study show how many residents need the program?

Use administrative counts or a suitable quantitative design for scale. Qualitative research can explain who is being missed, what barriers are at work and which assumptions the counts leave open.

When should we bring in an outside evaluator?

Consider an outside evaluator when you need independent findings or a study that can test what caused a result. If you first need to understand participant experiences, a narrower qualitative study can identify which questions and measures the evaluation should use.
