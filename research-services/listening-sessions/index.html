Prefer to read?

How can a listening session inform a decision without overpromising? Give the conversation a clear purpose and an honest boundary.

Before inviting people, explain what is open to change, what is fixed, and who makes the decision. Listening is not a promise to adopt every suggestion.

During the session, invite different accounts and ask neutral follow-up questions. A quiet room does not necessarily mean agreement, and the loudest voice is not the whole community.

Afterward, distinguish what people said from the team’s interpretation. Note whose perspectives were heard and the limits of the session. It is not a representative poll.

Then close the loop. Explain what happens next and how input will be considered. A clear update can connect the conversation to the organization’s actual decision process.

Direct answer

What is a community listening session?

A community listening session is a facilitated conversation that gives people a structured way to describe experiences, concerns and ideas related to a defined issue or decision.

It is not a vote or a representative poll. It may reveal what an organization had not considered, where information is unclear and how a proposed action is experienced. The value depends on who is invited, what can change and how the team interprets and responds to the input.

For a broader participation-to-decision plan, see community engagement research. This page focuses on the session itself and its facilitation.

Plan a community listening session from invitation to follow-up

A community listening session is a facilitated discussion where residents or people affected by a decision describe their experiences and priorities. It works best when the host names the question, gives people a fair way to contribute and tells them how they will hear back. Use the agenda and example prompts below to plan a session around a specific decision.

01Community listening session agenda

What should a 60-minute listening session agenda include?

Here is an illustrative schedule. Spend 10 minutes introducing the decision and what is open to input, 35 minutes listening to two or three prompts, 10 minutes reading back themes and checking what you missed, and 5 minutes naming the next decision and when participants will hear back.

Start by explaining how comments will be captured. Build a simple community listening session toolkit with the invitation, time blocks, facilitator prompts, note-taking sheet and report-back date. Allow more time for interpretation, access needs or smaller group discussion. If your team needs research across several decisions or many channels, use a community engagement plan to coordinate the wider work.

02Community listening session questions

What should a facilitator ask the community?

Invite people to describe something that happened. Try “Where did you get stuck?”, “What did you need at that point?” and “Who might have a different experience?” A follow-up such as “What would that look like in practice?” helps turn a broad preference into something the host can evaluate.

Agree in advance how the facilitator will make space for quieter people and handle off-topic issues. Give people a written way to add something they would rather not say aloud. For online sessions, explain who reads chat, whether chat is saved and how written input will be considered. EPA guidance on public participation.

03Listening session summary and report-back

What belongs in a listening session summary?

Capture the question, participation approach, repeated themes, material disagreements and perspectives you may have missed. Name the person or team responsible for the next decision. If a proposal cannot change because it is outside the decision, explain that constraint in the report-back and show which input still influenced the work.

For example, if residents ask for longer hours and the current budget is fixed, the agency could explain whether it will evaluate staggered hours and by when. Publishing a record of the next step makes feedback easier to follow. City of San Diego guide to reporting back.

A short public update could say, “Residents asked for later appointments. The service team will compare staggered hours at two locations and post the options before the next community meeting.” Include the date and where people can read the update. If the team needs more time, post a revised date and say what it is waiting to learn.

Copyable “what we heard” report template

  1. You said: [Theme, how we heard it and whose perspective may be missing.]
  2. What changed: [Action taken, with a reason tied to the input.]
  3. Still open or outside scope: [What remains unresolved or who has authority, with a reason.]
  4. Next update: [Owner, date and where to find it.]

For example, if families raise a transportation barrier, the team can publish what it heard, which meeting times it changed, who is reviewing transportation options, and the date and place for the next update.

Share an accessible public version through the channels people used to participate. Provide a way to flag an omission or correction and protect identities when summarizing small groups.

More questions before you start

Should we hire a facilitator when we already have a meeting host?

Consider it when the host owns the decision or needs to participate in the discussion. Agree on whether the facilitator is responsible for the agenda, live discussion and summary, or only for running the conversation. Assign each of the other tasks separately.

How do you plan an accessible virtual listening session?

Send clear joining instructions and explain how to request access or language support. Offer a written channel or another contribution method where feasible. Assign someone to help with joining and monitor written input so the facilitator can stay with the conversation.

What do we do when turnout is low?

Document who was reached and identify the missing perspectives. A smaller session can surface useful examples. Decide whether a second session, targeted interviews or another contribution channel will help before drawing broader conclusions.

Set the boundary first

Three questions before inviting anyone.

Good facilitation begins before the room opens.

01 / Decision

What is genuinely open?

Explain the choice the organization faces and which parts of a policy, plan or investment can still be shaped.

02 / People

Who needs access?

Consider who is affected, whose perspective is often absent and what timing, language or format may keep people out.

03 / Use

What happens after?

Decide how input will be recorded, synthesized and communicated back, including the limits of what it can determine.

Choose the format

Listening session or focus group?

A listening session often centers an organization's need to hear experiences and concerns about a public matter. A focus group is a more tightly structured qualitative method that explores a defined research question through group interaction. They can overlap in format but should not be described as interchangeable.

Do not overstate the evidence.

Neither an open forum nor a small facilitated group is a population sample by default. If the buyer needs prevalence estimates, that calls for a separately designed quantitative study.

Buyer questions

Listening-session questions, answered.

Who should be invited?

Start with people affected by the decision and groups whose perspective may be missing. The invitation plan should match the purpose and practical access needs.

Should the agency promise to adopt every idea?

No. Explain how input can influence the decision and who makes it. A clear boundary respects participants more than an unrealistic promise.

Can a session be virtual?

PRC moderates virtually and in person. Choose based on audience access, topic and facilitation needs.

What should the report include?

Agree deliverables in scope. A useful synthesis distinguishes participant accounts, interpretation, limitations and what decision-makers will consider next.

Practical decision aid

Give the session a clear opening and a useful ending.

A session should explain why people have been invited, how their contributions will be used and what happens afterward. Those expectations are part of the design, not a closing administrative note.

Before the room opens

Confirm the purpose, participants, access arrangements and decision owner. Decide how comments will be recorded and how any recording or confidentiality limits will be explained.

During the conversation

Use neutral prompts, invite different experiences and allow disagreement. Clarify an account without forcing consensus or treating the most confident speaker as the whole community.

After the session

Separate participant accounts from the team’s interpretation. Report themes, differences and limits, then explain the next decision and the route for an update.

What to clarify before commissioning

Plan for silence and disagreement. A quiet room may reflect unclear questions or participation barriers rather than agreement. Conflicting views should be described with context, not averaged into a false consensus. When attendance is self-selected, record who was reached and avoid claiming that the session speaks for everyone. The broader engagement plan should decide how missing perspectives can be heard.
