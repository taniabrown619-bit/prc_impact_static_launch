Direct answer

What is stakeholder research?

Stakeholder research is a structured way to learn how people with a role in or experience of a decision understand the issue. It can use interviews, small groups or listening sessions, depending on the question and the relationship among participants.

The result should help a team see where priorities align, where friction remains and what information is missing. It does not turn every stakeholder's view into a vote, and it should not present a few interviews as a representative survey.

Stakeholder map

Begin with roles, then ask better questions.

People affected

What changes in their experience if the decision goes forward? Which voices are often harder to reach?

People implementing

What practical constraints, handoffs and risks do staff or delivery partners see?

People deciding

What evidence does leadership need, and what parts of the choice are genuinely open?

For a broader public participation process, use community engagement research. This page focuses on mapping and interpreting stakeholder perspectives.

Research sequence

From names on a list to useful insight.

01

Frame the decision

Document the question the team needs to answer and when it must act.

02

Map perspectives

Identify affected people, decision makers, implementers and partners without assuming one person speaks for a whole group.

03

Listen with structure

Use a discussion guide that leaves room for concerns and examples the team did not predict.

04

Show differences

Report patterns, disagreements, blind spots and what remains uncertain before recommendations are made.

The GOV.UK service manual recommends planning interviews around research questions and using open, neutral prompts. It is general methods guidance, not evidence about PRC's outcomes.

Buyer questions

What should the research answer?

How is stakeholder research different from community engagement?

Stakeholder research maps and interprets perspectives connected to a decision. Community engagement may include a wider participation process with public influence and feedback commitments. A project may need both.

Do we need interviews with every stakeholder?

Not necessarily. The design should explain why each perspective is included and what gaps remain. Qualitative findings should describe depth and context, not imply statistical coverage.

What should we bring to a scoping call?

Bring the decision timeline, known stakeholder groups, existing evidence, open questions and any access or confidentiality constraints.
