Prefer to read?

How does stakeholder research turn interviews into usable direction? A list of names is like a map with no terrain. It tells you who is on paper, but not where the difficult crossings are.

First name the decision. Then distinguish people affected by it, people who will implement it and people who will make it. Ask open questions about priorities, constraints and what each group thinks is being missed.

Imagine a public institution planning a service change. Staff may see an operational bottleneck, partners may see a handoff problem and community members may see an access barrier. The report should show those differences, not force a false consensus.

A few interviews provide depth, not a population percentage. PRC Impact helps organizations connect stakeholder perspectives to decisions. Bring your decision and timeline to prcimpact.com, or call 334-429-6994.

Direct answer

What is stakeholder research?

Stakeholder research is a structured way to learn how people with a role in or experience of a decision understand the issue. It can use interviews, small groups or listening sessions, depending on the question and the relationship among participants.

The result should help a team see where priorities align, where friction remains and what information is missing. It does not turn every stakeholder's view into a vote, and it should not present a few interviews as a representative survey.

PRC Impact field guide

Map stakeholders and turn interviews into decisions

Stakeholder research helps a public-sector or coalition team compare how people experience a problem, deliver a program or make decisions about it. Map those roles before collecting interviews so the study can explain where perspectives diverge.

01Stakeholder mapping and role analysis

Who belongs in the stakeholder research map?

Start with three questions. Who experiences the consequences? Who carries out the work? Who can approve, fund or block a change? Include people who know the process well but may be absent from formal meetings. Record what the team needs to learn from each role.

For example, a referral process may involve service users, frontline staff, community partners and program leaders. Their observations are useful for different reasons. If the goal is wider participation in a public decision, use community engagement research to plan that effort.

02Stakeholder interview design and questions

What should interviews ask across different roles?

Use a common prompt in your stakeholder interviews, such as “Where does this referral become harder to complete?” Then ask questions specific to each role. A participant may remember waiting for a call. A frontline worker may describe incomplete information at handoff. A manager may see delays in aggregate reporting. Ask each person for a recent example and what happened next.

Use the in-depth interview guide to design neutral prompts and follow the individual’s account. CDC qualitative research methods.

03Stakeholder findings and decision mapping

How should a research team report conflicting accounts?

Organize results by issue and role. Record agreement, disagreements and what each person can see from their position. Where leaders believe referrals are completed and residents say they received no answer, check if the parties use different definitions of “completed.”

Report what the team can change now, which questions require more evidence and who owns each decision. Describe important perspectives the study did not reach so the sponsor knows what remains uncertain.

More questions before you start

Should interview notes identify individual stakeholders?

Agree with the project team on who can see raw notes, where names are necessary and which details will be omitted from shared findings. Be especially careful with small groups where a role alone may identify someone.

What if one stakeholder group is unavailable?

Document the gap and what it means for interpretation. If that group may have different firsthand knowledge or a distinct role in the decision, plan a follow-up channel before treating the study as complete.

Stakeholder map

Begin with roles, then ask better questions.

People affected

People implementing

Research sequence

From names on a list to useful insight.

01

Frame the decision

Document the question the team needs to answer and when it must act.

02

Map perspectives

Identify affected people, decision makers, implementers and partners without assuming one person speaks for a whole group.

03

Listen with structure

Use a discussion guide that leaves room for concerns and examples the team did not predict.

04

Show differences

Report patterns, disagreements, blind spots and what remains uncertain before recommendations are made.

Buyer questions

What should the research answer?

How is stakeholder research different from community engagement?

Stakeholder research maps and interprets perspectives connected to a decision. Community engagement may include a wider participation process with public influence and feedback commitments. A project may need both.

Do we need interviews with every stakeholder?

Not necessarily. The design should explain why each perspective is included and what gaps remain. Qualitative findings should describe depth and context, not imply statistical coverage.

What should we bring to a scoping call?

Bring the decision timeline, known stakeholder groups, existing evidence, open questions and any access or confidentiality constraints.
