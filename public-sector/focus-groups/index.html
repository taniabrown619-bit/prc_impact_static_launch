Full video transcript

Prefer to read?

When should a government agency use focus groups? Picture a draft public service as a map. A survey may show how many people chose a route. A focus group can reveal why the signs confused them.

Use government focus groups when you need people to discuss a shared issue, compare experiences or explain how they understand a proposed change. Begin with the public decision, then decide who needs to be heard and what participants can actually influence.

For example, an agency considering a new service notice could ask participants what the notice says in their own words, what is unclear and what information is missing. Those answers can guide a revision. They do not estimate the views of all residents.

Plan access, consent and reporting before invitations go out. PRC Impact helps public organizations scope and lead qualitative research. Discuss the decision at prcimpact.com, or call 334-429-6994.

Direct answer

When do government focus groups fit?

Use focus groups when an agency needs to hear people discuss a shared issue, compare experiences or react to draft information together. Group interaction can reveal language, points of confusion and differences in reasoning that a questionnaire may miss.

They are a qualitative method. A small group does not estimate how many residents hold a view. If the decision requires population percentages, plan a separate representative measurement approach. The CDC's qualitative methods guidance explains the distinction between structured group discussion and other forms of evidence.

Procurement-ready thinking

Put five things in the research brief.

01

The decision

What policy, service, plan or investment will the findings inform? Who has decision authority?

02

The public's role

What can participants influence, and what has already been decided?

03

The people

Which affected groups need to be heard, and what access barriers should recruitment address?

04

The safeguards

Who handles consent, confidentiality, records and participant support?

05

The deliverable

What themes, differences, limits and next-step recommendations would help the agency act?

Method choice

Focus group, public forum or interview?

Focus group

Use a recruited, moderated discussion to explore how a defined audience reasons together.

Public forum

Use a more open setting when the agency needs broad participation or public exchange about a decision.

Individual interview

Use a one-to-one format when the topic is sensitive or individual circumstances need more depth.

For the broader participation plan, read community engagement research. The EPA public participation guide advises matching the participation level to the public's real influence.

Agency questions

Questions that improve the RFP.

How many groups should an agency request?

There is no useful preset number. Scope groups around the audiences and differences the decision requires, then describe what the design can and cannot support.

Can a focus group replace a public hearing?

A focus group answers a different research question. It does not satisfy any public-hearing obligation. The agency's procurement and legal teams should determine required formal processes.

How should findings be reported?

Summarize recurring themes, meaningful disagreement, participant context and limits. Keep participant quotations and confidential information within the agreed review and consent process.

Planning guide

How agencies can use focus groups for public decisions

A government focus group brings selected participants together to discuss a specific policy, service or communication question. It can help a municipal, county or state team understand what people find confusing, what tradeoffs matter and what the agency should investigate before deciding.

Match the group to the government decision

For a city service, recruit people who have used or tried to use it and compare what happened at different steps. For a county or state policy change, consider groups based on how the change affects participants. If staff knowledge is important, hear from staff separately where their role could change how residents respond.

Choose what to show residents

An illustrative example is a county revising a recycling collection notice. Show residents the same draft schedule, ask them to find their collection day, and listen for differences in how they interpret exceptions. The findings can guide revisions and show where the agency needs further evidence. The CDC qualitative research manual explains how open-ended research helps teams explore the reasons behind responses.

Put a usable deliverable in the RFP

Ask bidders to show how they would turn a comment into a finding and then a decision. For the recycling example, request a table with the part of the notice tested, the misunderstanding, the groups that raised it and the proposed revision. Ask the vendor to identify gaps in coverage and explain which findings can go in a public summary. San Diego’s public engagement guide describes ways agency staff can capture focus group input.
