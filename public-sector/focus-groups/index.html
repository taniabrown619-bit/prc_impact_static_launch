Full video transcript

Prefer to read?

When should a government agency use focus groups? Picture a draft public service as a map. A survey may show how many people chose a route. A focus group can reveal why the signs confused them.

Use government focus groups when you need people to discuss a shared issue, compare experiences or explain how they understand a proposed change. Begin with the public decision, then decide who needs to be heard and what participants can actually influence.

For example, an agency considering a new service notice could ask participants what the notice says in their own words, what is unclear and what information is missing. Those answers can guide a revision. They do not estimate the views of all residents.

Plan access, consent and reporting before invitations go out. PRC Impact helps public organizations scope and lead qualitative research. Discuss the decision at prcimpact.com, or call 334-429-6994.

Direct answer

When do government focus groups fit?

Use focus groups when an agency needs to hear people discuss a shared issue, compare experiences or react to draft information together. Group interaction can reveal language, points of confusion and differences in reasoning that a questionnaire may miss.

They are a qualitative method. A small group does not estimate how many residents hold a view. If the decision requires population percentages, plan a separate representative measurement approach. The CDC's qualitative methods guidance explains the distinction between structured group discussion and other forms of evidence.

Government focus groups for agency decisions

Government focus groups help agencies understand how selected residents, service users or staff interpret a specific policy or service. Start with the agency decision and build the recruitment, discussion guide and reporting around it.

01Municipal and state agency focus groups

When should a public agency commission a focus group?

Use a focus group when participants can help your team understand why a service is difficult to use, how people read proposed instructions, or where they see a tradeoff in a policy option. A city could explore confusion about a change in collection days. A county could compare different explanations of where residents should find service information. A state agency could explore how people understand a proposed program change.

Write down who owns the decision, what can change and which perspective is missing. For broad participation, add a distinct community engagement process. For a sensitive individual experience, consider interviews. City of San Diego focus group guidance.

02Resident focus group recruitment

How do you recruit residents for useful public sector research?

Use experience with the decision as the first screening criterion. To study a notice, recruit people who found and used the information, people who misread it and people who never saw it. Recruiting only from an existing email list may miss the very residents whose experience explains the gap. Decide which languages, access needs and ways of participating to support before invitations go out.

When staff or partner organizations may influence what residents say, give them a separate discussion. Ask the research team to report who was reached in general terms and which perspectives were missing. Use the differences to identify changes worth testing. CDC qualitative research methods.

03Government focus group RFP and reporting

What should a government focus group RFP ask vendors to deliver?

Ask the vendor to describe the recruitment plan, moderation, analysis and reporting as separate responsibilities. Name the draft or decision the agency will provide, its due date, who will approve discussion materials and whether the agency needs an internal memo, a public summary or both. State accessibility, recording and material-handling requirements through the agency’s normal review process.

For a recycling notice, request a table with the instruction tested, common readings, audiences that raised each reading, and the proposed change. Ask for contradictory examples and unresolved questions alongside repeated themes so staff can tell which revisions are ready and which need another round.

More questions before you start

How should an agency decide how many groups to run?

Start with the perspectives you need to compare. If a service works differently for new and experienced users, give each group a chance to describe its own experience. Ask the vendor what another group would help distinguish and how it would report gaps if recruitment falls short.

Can residents help test a policy that is already decided?

They can help test how an agency explains or implements it, if those choices are still open. Tell participants what has been decided, what feedback can influence and how the agency will use it. EPA public participation guidance.

Can these findings tell us how many residents agree?

The findings show how selected participants reasoned and where their interpretations differed. A population estimate requires a separately designed quantitative study. Use the groups to improve the questions or explain patterns the agency measures elsewhere.

Procurement-ready thinking

Put five things in the research brief.

01

The decision

What policy, service, plan or investment will the findings inform? Who has decision authority?

02

The public's role

What can participants influence, and what has already been decided?

03

The people

Which affected groups need to be heard, and what access barriers should recruitment address?

04

The safeguards

Who handles consent, confidentiality, records and participant support?

05

The deliverable

What themes, differences, limits and next-step recommendations would help the agency act?

Method choice

Focus group, public forum or interview?

Focus group

Use a recruited, moderated discussion to explore how a defined audience reasons together.

Public forum

Use a more open setting when the agency needs broad participation or public exchange about a decision.

Individual interview

Use a one-to-one format when the topic is sensitive or individual circumstances need more depth.

For the broader participation plan, read community engagement research. The EPA public participation guide advises matching the participation level to the public's real influence.

Agency questions

Questions that improve the RFP.

How many groups should an agency request?

There is no useful preset number. Scope groups around the audiences and differences the decision requires, then describe what the design can and cannot support.

Can a focus group replace a public hearing?

A focus group answers a different research question. It does not satisfy any public-hearing obligation. The agency's procurement and legal teams should determine required formal processes.

How should findings be reported?

Summarize recurring themes, meaningful disagreement, participant context and limits. Keep participant quotations and confidential information within the agreed review and consent process.
