What questions should a school ask families if it wants to improve how it communicates?

Think of a parent survey like a flashlight. Aim it at one decision and you can see what’s getting in the way. Point it everywhere and you may still leave with guesses.

Imagine a school that wants more families to use its after-school program. Ask whether they saw the invitation first. Then ask what made signing up harder. A late reminder and an inconvenient pickup time are two different problems.

The Kansas State Department of Education’s family engagement survey is one example of asking about specific experiences, including school communication. Test your own questions with families before launch. Offer more than one way to respond and check whose voices you missed.

Tell families what you heard and what you’ll try next. If your district needs help planning the research, visit prcimpact.com or call PRC Impact at (334) 429-6994.

01 / Plan

Start with the decision

Write one sentence that tells families why you’re asking and what can change. For example, “We’re asking families how they learned about the after-school program so we can decide when and how to send next term’s invitation.” Name the staff member or team responsible and pick a date to share results before the survey opens.

Use a family engagement survey when you need comparable answers to the same questions. If you still don’t know why families miss updates, start with a few short interviews to hear how they actually get information. Use those answers to write better choices for the survey.

This guide covers routine school programs and communication. If you’re planning public input and updates for an already funded construction project, use the school capital project engagement guide.

02 / Questionnaire

Sample parent survey questions for schools

These original questions use an after-school program as the example. Keep the timeframe and program name consistent, then choose the questions that will inform your decision.

  1. In the past month, did you see an invitation for the after-school program?Yes, no, or not sure. If the answer is no, skip the next two questions and ask about barriers to signing up.
  2. Where did you first see or hear about it?Offer the school’s actual channels, such as email, the school app, a printed note, or another person. Include “other” and “don’t remember.”
  3. How easy was it to find the signup instructions?Use a balanced scale from very difficult to very easy. Include an option for families who did not look for the instructions.
  4. What, if anything, made it harder for your family to sign up?Offer separate choices for timing, transportation, language, technology and unclear instructions. Include “nothing,” “other” and “prefer not to answer.”
  5. How would you prefer to receive the next program update?List the channels the school can use. Let families choose more than one if the team can act on that feedback.
  6. What is one change that would make this program easier for your family to use?Make the response optional and ask families to leave out names and student information.

For a broader instrument, see the Kansas State Department of Education’s family engagement survey. It covers experiences such as whether staff welcome families and communicate in their preferred language.

03 / Fieldwork

Reach families the usual channels miss

  1. Test the draft. Ask a few caregivers with different language and access needs to take it on a phone or on paper. Have them tell you what a question means and why they picked an answer. Revise confusing words, answer choices and skip instructions.
  2. Offer a second route. Pair the usual digital invite with paper or a way to respond by phone where feasible. Send the same purpose and deadline through both channels. Have translations checked with people who use them.
  3. Track who you’re hearing from. Decide whether the intended response is one per household or one per school. While the survey is open, check which schools, grade bands or language groups are underrepresented where the collection plan allows it. Use targeted reminders while protecting individual responses.

Keep identifiers out unless the research needs them. Explain who can see responses and how they will be used. If you invite volunteers to speak with staff later, collect their contact details through a separate opt-in where practical. See AAPOR’s survey design and reporting guidance for coverage, pretesting and transparent reporting.

04 / Follow through

Turn answers into a school decision

Start with who was invited, how they could reply and who answered. When the survey is voluntary, describe what respondents reported. Compare patterns across schools or channels only when there are enough replies to make the comparison useful and protect privacy. Report the number of answers behind any percentage, and summarize open comments without identifying families.

Illustrative example

Families who found the signup link say the instructions were clear, but many respondents say they never saw it. The school can test an earlier reminder through an additional channel. If follow-up conversations point to pickup time instead, the team should investigate scheduling before deciding what to change.

Send a short update through the same channels used for the invitation. Say what you heard, what the team will try, what is still undecided, and when families can expect another update.

Common questions

Before you send it

How long should a school parent survey be?

Keep the questions needed for the decision and time the draft with a few families. Share the tested completion time in the invitation. Remove questions the team can answer from information it already has.

Can a parent survey tell us what all families think?

A voluntary survey tells you what the people who responded reported. If some schools or groups are hard to reach, report the gap and seek their input before drawing a conclusion about the district as a whole.

When should we follow up with interviews?

Follow up when a response points to a barrier without explaining it. If families select “timing,” interviews can help you distinguish a short registration window from a pickup time that conflicts with work.
